Exploratory data analysis for the AML Alert Prioritization task

Alerts get escalated when cash is large for that customer

We explored 14,000 labelled AML alerts and 7 million transactions. The burst of activity that fires an alert looks the same for escalated and dismissed cases. What separates them is how big a customer's cash deposits and card payments are compared with their own bank transfers.

Escalation rate by how large cash deposits are relative to the customer's own bank transfers

14,000 training alerts split into five equal groups, from smallest to largest relative cash size

  • 17.2% of alerts escalated
  • 2.7× escalation rate, lowest to highest group
  • 0.646 ROC-AUC on a 2,000-alert holdout

The task and how we approached it

A monitoring unit receives automated alerts built from each customer's transaction history. Specialists escalate some alerts and dismiss the rest. We predict the probability that each of 6,000 hidden test alerts is escalated, scored by ROC-AUC. A good ranking lets analysts open the riskiest alerts first.

We treated the EDA as a series of hypotheses. Each observation became a candidate feature, and a feature was only kept if it improved a fixed validation score.

The data: one alert, hundreds of transactions

The data is relational. Each alert in the signals table links to many rows in the transactions table through signal_id. The data is synthetic and localised for the hackathon; it contains no real customer records.

Alerts are dated from 1 January 2025 to 31 December 2026. Every alert carries exactly 180 days of history, so transactions start in July 2024. No value is missing. A typical alert has 461 transactions, with a range from 1 to 2,279. Escalated alerts have slightly more (median 493 against 455).

The target: 17% escalated, steady over time

2,405 of 14,000 training alerts (17.2%) were escalated. The rate stays between 16% and 19% in every quarter, so there is no trend for the model to chase. The test set has the same quarterly mix as the training set, matching to the fourth decimal place. That tells us the organisers sampled by date, so a random stratified split is a fair way to validate.

Activity over time, and the 180-second burst

Looking at when transactions happen relative to the alert revealed the most striking structure in the data. Almost every alert (98.9%) has a dense burst of transactions in the final 180 seconds before the alert timestamp: about 39 on average, against a background of 0.04 per hour. The burst holds 7.9% of all transactions and looks identical in the test set (39.3 per alert).

Nothing happens after the alert day, so there is no leakage from the future. Only 0.06% of transactions fall on the alert date itself.

Outside the burst, activity fades steadily as the alert approaches, from about 22 transactions a week four to five months out to 7 in the final week. Escalated customers are consistently about 5% more active in every week. Their typical transaction size is also consistently smaller, and the gap does not widen near the alert.

The burst is what triggers an alert, not what decides it. Its size barely differs between classes (AUC 0.51). The difference is a stable trait of the customer, visible across all 180 days.

This finding shaped our features. We split every alert into three segments: the burst, the 180-day history and the rare post-alert rows. We then summarised the history as stable customer traits rather than recent change.

Incoming versus outgoing, and transaction types

Three in four transactions are incoming. Cards and bank transfers make up 93% of all activity, cash 6% and cross-border payments under 0.5%. The mix is almost the same for both classes: 75.3% incoming for escalated against 75.7% for dismissed, and type shares differ by at most 0.2 points.

The mix does not separate the classes, but it strongly shapes transaction size. Cross-border payments sit about 2 standard units above incoming card payments, and outgoing transactions are larger than incoming ones within every type. A raw average across a customer's transactions therefore mostly measures their payment mix. That is why we compare sizes within each type and direction.

Transaction sizes

The size indicator is standardised, so it measures relative rather than absolute amounts. Pick a type and direction to compare the full distributions. Most panels overlap almost perfectly. Bank transfers are the exception: escalated customers' transfers are shifted towards smaller values.

We also checked for repeated exact amounts, a classic sign of structuring. Only four values repeat. They are per-type maximums, such as the card ceiling that appears 115 times, not round-sum deposits.

Testing AML red flags against the labels

We took the red flags that regulators such as FATF and FinCEN list for banks and turned each one into a measurement per alert. Then we checked how the escalation rate changes across five equal-sized groups of that measurement. All panels share one scale starting at zero, so the slopes are directly comparable. AUC shows how well each measurement ranks alerts on its own, whichever direction it points; 0.50 is random.

Small, uniform bank transfers are the strongest single flag: escalation falls from 22% to 13% as typical transfer size grows. Activity volume, pass-through and cross-border share add weaker, positive signals. Night-time activity carries nothing, because transaction hours in this dataset are perfectly uniform.

The key pattern: size relative to the customer's own habits

On their own, cash-deposit size (AUC 0.51) and card-payment size (0.50) look useless. Yet our model gave them some of its largest weights. The explanation is that the red flag is relative. A cash deposit matters when it is large compared with what the same customer usually moves by bank transfer.

This matches FATF's description of cash activity "out of step" with a customer's profile. It also turns two useless measurements into the best single predictor we found.

What we ruled out

Several promising ideas did not hold up. We report them because they show which directions not to pursue and why our pipeline looks the way it does.

  • Time of day and weekday. Each hour holds exactly 4.2% of transactions and each weekday 14.3%. Timestamps are uniformly random, so night or weekend features cannot carry signal.
  • Linking alerts to shared customers. Real triage systems gain a lot from a customer's past escalations. Here, only 2 of 10 million transactions appear under two different alerts, so each alert is effectively a separate customer.
  • Leakage through the alert ID. The ID number predicts nothing (AUC 0.499).
  • Simple yes/no rules. Flags such as a cash or cross-border payment inside the trigger burst, a very young account or a short history moved the escalation rate by 2 points at most. The label behaves like a noisy score, not a rule.
  • A transaction-level model. We trained a model on single transactions labelled with their alert's outcome. At first it scored 0.70 on training alerts but 0.51 on new ones, because it memorised alerts through alert-level inputs. After we removed those inputs it scored 0.56 and added nothing to the main model, so we dropped it.
  • Unsupervised anomaly detection. Many banks rank alerts by how unusual a customer looks. Isolation Forest (0.52), Mahalanobis distance (0.51) and Local Outlier Factor (0.50) scored close to random: an unusual customer is not the same as an escalated one here.
  • Short-window velocity rules. Counts, sums and in/out ratios over the last hour, day and week scored 0.59 on their own and slightly lowered the main model when added. Short windows mostly capture the trigger burst, which looks the same for both classes.

Results

The final model is a rank average of four models from different families. It scores 0.650 in cross-validation and 0.646 on the 2,000-alert holdout, which none of our decisions had touched. The two numbers agree, so the model is not overfitting. With 2,000 alerts the holdout's 95% bootstrap interval is 0.614 to 0.676. For the submission we retrained on all 14,000 alerts.

Conclusion

  1. The alert trigger is not the story. Every alert comes with a 180-second burst of about 39 transactions, and it looks the same for escalated and dismissed cases.
  2. Escalation follows the customer, not the moment. Escalated customers differ consistently across the whole 180-day history, not just before the alert.
  3. The strongest red flag is relative. Cash deposits and card payments that are large compared with the customer's own bank transfers raise escalation from 10% to 27%. Of the standard red flags taken one at a time, small and uniform bank transfers are the strongest.
  4. Simple beat complex. 50 per-cell statistics with shrinkage and a linear model outperformed 291 features with boosted trees. This suggests the labels are a noisy weighted score of customer traits.
  5. Diverse models, honest estimate. Of 100 methods compared, a rank average of four models from different families worked best. CV 0.650 and holdout 0.646 agree. Reviewing the top 30% of alerts catches about 48% of escalations, 1.6 times what random review would.
